# 2026-04-20 — Agent Pattern Scan

## TL;DR
Block just baked a proactive agent into the Square POS that watches inventory, scheduling, and marketing and proposes specific actions with one-tap approval. For every SMB sector Block won't serve — dental, vet, law, music studios, solo accountants — the pattern says the same thing: the data is already in your vertical SaaS; the piece you're missing is the agent that reads it every morning and hands you the send.

## Patterns

### 1. A proactive co-pilot baked into the operational SaaS
*(tags: `retail`, `dental-and-vet-clinics`, `music-studios`, `accountants`, `insurance-agencies`)*

Block launched Managerbot last week, an agent that runs inside Square and acts on the seller's data without being asked ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/data/block-introduces-managerbot-a-proactive-square-ai-agent-and-the-clearest), [Shopifreaks](https://www.shopifreaks.com/block-launches-managerbot-a-proactive-ai-agent-for-square-sellers-that-manages-inventory-scheduling-and-marketing-autonomously/), [Square support doc](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/8617-use-managerbot-to-manage-business-tasks-and-insights)). It monitors stock levels and sales velocity, pulls external signals like weather and local events, detects emerging problems, and surfaces recommendations with proposed actions. The seller approves or edits; every write is gated. Today it runs across three domains — inventory forecasting, shift scheduling, and marketing campaigns — and Block is framing it as the virtual COO for the 4 million SMBs in Square's book.

The interesting part isn't that a POS added AI. It's that Block decided the unit of delivery is a proactive agent inside the workflow the operator already lives in, not a standalone tool, not a chatbot, not a dashboard. That shape is the one to copy.

Every SMB vertical has a Square equivalent — the one system the business actually runs on. For a dental practice, it's Dentrix or Eaglesoft. The analogous agent watches the recall list, the provider calendar, and the no-show history, and every morning drafts three things in the MA's queue: recall texts to the patients who've aged past six months with a specific suggested slot, insurance-renewal pings to patients whose coverage changes at quarter-end, and unbilled-procedure nudges on anything approaching the timely-filing cliff. Stack is the Dentrix API or a CSV extract, Claude, Twilio, and a one-screen approval UI. Around $300–500 a month. Replaces the front-desk afternoon where recall happens if there's time. Pitch: your PMS already has the recall list; what it doesn't have is the thing that reads it every morning and hands you the send.

For a vet clinic, it's ezyVet vaccination reminders crossed with upcoming travel-certificate windows and a rabies-boosters-overdue list. For a two-location music studio, it's Opus1 enrollment data crossed against teacher availability and seasonal recital deadlines — the agent notices the flute student whose attendance dropped 40 percent in March and drafts a re-engagement call for the director to make. For a solo accountant running Karbon, it's the client list filtered by missing docs thirty days before deadline, pre-drafted chase emails per client referencing the specific schedule or form outstanding. Same mechanism each time, different vertical SaaS on the input side.

### 2. The voice-commanded outreach agent
*(tags: `trades`, `landscaping`, `real-estate`, `music-studios`)*

Flow AI — a real estate outreach product — moved its campaign engine onto n8n and built a mechanism worth stealing ([n8n case study](https://n8n.io/case-studies/flow-ai/)). An agent says into their phone, "I'm hosting an open house Saturday, invite everyone in my contacts." ElevenLabs converts speech into structured input and hits an n8n webhook. Workflows query the Postgres contact database, verify opt-in status and 10DLC/CAN-SPAM compliance, generate personalized content with Claude, and send through SMS providers or Mailgun. Everything is logged. End-to-end in under 60 seconds. A campaign that used to need a full-time assistant now runs from a voice command between showings.

The trigger is the operator's voice. That matters more than the stack. SMB owners are rarely at a desk. They're driving, on a ladder, behind a chair, at a recital. Most of the day, their input device is their phone and their thumbs are busy. Voice collapses the "when I get back to the office I'll send that" into now.

At a landscaping company, the owner says "text everyone on this week's spring cleanup list that rain pushed us to Thursday." The agent pulls the scheduled jobs from Jobber, filters to the affected customers, sends personalized SMS with the new slot, and logs the send against each job record. Stack is Vapi or ElevenLabs plus Claude plus Jobber's API plus Twilio, running on n8n or Make. Around $60–100 a month. Replaces the 30 minutes of Sunday texting the owner was doing from bed. Pitch: you already drive between jobs talking to yourself; now your phone dispatches the outreach before you pull into the next driveway.

At a music studio, the director says "invite all my high school seniors to a June recital." The agent queries Opus1 or a Google Sheets roster, drafts parent-addressed emails with an RSVP link to a Google Form, and posts the RSVP list back into a shared sheet as they come in. Same thing for a real estate brokerage doing open houses, an HVAC shop doing a seasonal tune-up push, or a pest control company doing quarterly renewals. Around $50 a month.

### 3. Categorize once with an LLM, route deterministically after
*(tags: `accountants`, `insurance-agencies`, `law-firms`, `property-management`)*

On How I AI this month, JJ Englert from Tenex walked through how he built a Slack digest that handles his notification firehose ([Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/this-week-on-how-i-ai-claude-cowork)). The design choice is the takeaway. The agent doesn't classify every message with an LLM. It categorizes the buckets once — DMs, mentions, threads, channel mentions — then sub-routes into action, read, or FYI using deterministic rules generated from the LLM's pattern. Claude only runs on the genuinely ambiguous tail. The result is cheap, fast, and predictable.

Most SMB inbox-triage pitches fail because they try to send every email through an LLM. That's expensive, slow, and weird when the model has a bad day. Englert's shape is better: have the model design the taxonomy and the rules once, then let those rules handle 80 percent of the volume for fractions of a cent, and only call the model on the last 20 percent.

At a solo accountant, most client email is predictable by sender, subject pattern, and attachment type — W-2 uploads, quarterly estimate questions, K-1 questions, payroll escalations. Each one routes to a Gmail label with an owner and a pre-drafted reply stub. The LLM only reads the ambiguous messages, which at a 200-client book is maybe 15 a day. Stack is Gmail Apps Script plus Claude for the initial rule generation plus the Claude API for the tail. Around $40–80 a month. Replaces the partner's evening email triage and the Saturday catch-up session. Pitch: you don't need an AI looking at every email; you need one built once, then a router that costs pennies to run.

At a small independent insurance agency, the volume is certificate requests, claim acknowledgments, and policy-change questions. Deterministic rules handle certificate issuance — a pre-approved template, signed, attached, sent — and the LLM only intervenes on claims or endorsements with non-standard language. At a two-partner law firm, intake forms and scheduling questions route deterministically; only client-specific substantive emails get agent drafts. Same mechanism. Different vertical's email mix.
