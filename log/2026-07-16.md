# 2026-07-16 — Agent Pattern Scan

## TL;DR
Three patterns worth stealing. DoorDash's code reviewer is the interesting mechanism today: a cheap scout narrows the search space, then two expensive reviewers verify only the flagged spots. That two-tier "scout then expert" architecture is the copy target — it cuts cost and false positives on any high-volume review task, from tax return QA at an accounting firm to insurance claim review at a clinic.

## Patterns

### 1. Scout-then-expert two-tier review agent
*(tags: `accountants`, `dental-and-vet-clinics`, `insurance`, `professional-services`, `small-construction`)*

DoorDash [published](https://careersatdoordash.com/blog/how-we-learned-to-trust-our-ai-code-reviewer-at-doordash/) how their code-review agent works after rolling it out across 56 repos and 10,000+ pull requests a week. The interesting bit is the architecture, not the domain. A "lead scout" model reads the whole diff cheaply and flags a shortlist of suspicious spots. Two "deep reviewer" agents then look only at those spots with a bigger, slower model, verify or reject each concern, and post inline comments on the PR. In production DoorDash runs Claude Sonnet 4.6 as the scout and Claude Opus 4.8 as the deep reviewer, and reports 504 real findings at 53.6% weighted recall versus 164 findings and 30.7% recall for a single-pass GPT baseline — at roughly $3.91 per PR. They also built a benchmark called DashBench that replays historical PRs to score whether the agent surfaces findings humans actually acted on, which is what gave engineers enough trust to let the agent's comments show up next to their teammates'.

The reason this matters outside software is that lots of SMB work is "expensive expert eyes on high-volume routine artifacts." Accounting firms have exactly this shape during tax season: junior preparer submits a return, senior CPA has to review every single one line-by-line before it goes out. The trigger is a return marked "ready for review" in Drake, Lacerte, or UltraTax. A scout agent reads the whole return against the prior year and the source documents in Google Drive, then hands the CPA a two-page memo: "here are the seven line items where the numbers or classifications look wrong, with the underlying source cited." The CPA no longer eyeballs the full return; they check the flagged items. The pitch: "Your seniors stop scanning returns line-by-line during March and April, and start reviewing only the seven things per return that actually matter." The stack is a Claude API workflow reading return data via each tax platform's export, Google Drive for source docs, and posting the memo into the practice management task list. Dental and veterinary clinics have the same shape for insurance claim submissions in Dentrix or AVImark before a biller submits: scout flags the codes and attachments most likely to be denied, an expert model justifies or drops each, and only exceptions land in the biller's queue. Property and casualty insurance adjusters have it too, on inbound claims. The recurring generalizable trick is not "get an agent to review things," it's "use a cheap model to cut the search space so the expensive model only runs where it matters."

### 2. Custom agents replacing the SMB SaaS stack
*(tags: `professional-services`, `music-and-tutoring-schools`, `agencies`, `coaching`, `online-education`)*

Time [profiled](https://time.com/article/2026/05/14/ai-small-businesses-layoffs/) Spencer Handley, who runs Sonora — an online guitar school — and cut his team from 48 to 30 while keeping revenue flat by replacing HubSpot, Calendly, Vimeo, and DocuSign with custom agentic workflows built on Claude Opus 4.5. Concretely, the outreach team was eliminated: an outbound agent runs sequenced messages, personalizes off enrichment data, and books discovery calls without HubSpot sequences. The onboarding staff was eliminated: an onboarding agent takes a signed enrollment, provisions the student, schedules the intake session on the instructor's calendar, mails the welcome kit, and lands video lessons where Vimeo used to. The operations coordinator role was eliminated: an ops agent handles rescheduling, refunds, and enrollment changes over email using the same shared inboxes staff used to sit in. Annual savings run around $250K. Handley's phrase for the story is "pioneer species" — the model has just gotten good enough at long-running agentic work for a small business to replace category SaaS with custom agent workflows instead of stacking another subscription.

The generalizable pattern is: when a bundle of SaaS tools each cost $100–500/mo and you use maybe 10% of what they do, an agent can be the thin thing that does the 10% you actually use, with your CRM being a Google Sheet or a Postgres table you own. This lands hard in verticals where the SaaS is overkill. A tutoring franchise doesn't need HubSpot Sales Hub Pro — a small outbound agent reading a lead spreadsheet and drafting personalized parent emails, with a human clicking approve, does the job. A small marketing agency doesn't need Calendly Teams — an agent that watches an inbox for "can we grab time next week" and drafts a reply proposing three slots from the founder's Google Calendar is the whole product. The pitch to that agency owner: "You're paying $6K a year for Calendly, HubSpot, and Loom to do things a $500-a-month agent could handle better, because the agent knows about your specific clients and services and those tools don't." The stack for an SMB shop is Google Workspace, one vertical CRM that stays, and a Claude-driven backend replacing whatever middleware they used to string it together with.

### 3. Describe-a-process, get an approvable workflow
*(tags: `trades`, `restaurants`, `home-health`, `property-management`, `nonprofit`)*

Rewst, an automation vendor selling to MSPs who serve SMBs, [rolled out](https://www.channele2e.com/news/rewst-ai-agent-update-gives-msps-a-new-smb-automation-play) an AI Agent tool where the human types a description of a process ("when a new client signs the MSA in DocuSign, create their Microsoft 365 tenant, send them the onboarding email, and schedule the kickoff call") and the agent generates a full runnable workflow. The human sees the generated steps, edits any that are wrong, and approves — then it runs on trigger. The interesting shift is who is doing the authoring: previously an MSP had to have someone who knew Rewst's builder inside and out, and now the MSP owner or a technician can describe what should happen in English and get something they can eyeball before it goes live. The trigger becomes "I want to automate this" rather than "I have time this week to build in the automation tool."

This unlocks a set of sectors where SMB owners have the domain knowledge to describe their own automations but no interest in a visual workflow tool. Home health agencies could describe "when an aide clocks out of a visit in ClearCare, pull their visit notes, generate a family-friendly summary, email it to the emergency contact, and mark the visit ready-to-bill in the billing queue" and get a workflow they approve rather than pay a developer for. Restaurant groups could describe "each morning at 6 AM, check yesterday's Toast sales against the schedule, and if labor ran above 32%, text the GM the number and the two shifts that overshot" and get it deployed by lunch. For A3 Dev the read is: description-based authoring is coming to every workflow tool this year, so the value of "we can build workflows in n8n or Make" is dropping. What holds value is knowing which processes are worth automating for a given operator, what data feeds the trigger, and where the approval gate should sit — the meta-work above the workflow. The pitch is unchanged, the deliverable is faster to produce, and clients get to see the mechanism instead of just the outcome.
