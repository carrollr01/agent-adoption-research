The pattern worth acting on this week is the one Avoca has been quietly running for HVAC shops. Their voice agent is booking 400 calls a week at one operator — answering inbound and after-hours calls, asking qualifying questions, checking the tech calendar, honoring service-area and pricing rules, booking the slot, and confirming by SMS. The mechanism is already proven and the operationally identical sectors next door have almost none of it. A dental, vet, or small medical practice is the same shape of business: phone-heavy, calendar-constrained, expensive front desk. Roughly 200,000 practices in the US. That's the highest-leverage copy available in agent-land right now.

Below, in rough order of how excited I am about each one.

1. The Slack reaction is the work order (dental and vet clinics, small medical practices, accountants)

At Stripe, an engineer reacts to a Slack message with a specific emoji and an agent picks it up — reads the thread, spins up a dev environment, makes the code change, opens a PR that links back. One click, in the tool they already use. Stripe ships around 1,300 PRs a week this way. Steve Kaliski walked through it on How I AI on March 25. https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-stripe-built-minionsai-coding

The interesting part is not the code. It's the trigger. Every knowledge-work SMB has the same shape of problem — email comes in, sits for two days, the person who owned it has to reconstruct context before they can act. Drop this into a dental or vet office and the trigger is a Gmail label on a patient thread. The agent reads the thread, pulls the chart note from Dentrix or ezyVet, drafts a reply in the provider's voice as a Gmail draft, and pings the medical assistant. Stack is Gmail plus an Apps Script label watcher plus Claude plus a shim over the practice management software. Around $200–400 a month. Replaces about 90 minutes a day of front-desk triage. The pitch: label the message, get back a chart-aware draft; the provider signs or edits. Same shape works at a two-partner CPA firm — label a client email, get a draft grounded in the prior-year return from Karbon or Canopy.

2. A Monday-morning text that does the work of a Monday-morning report (restaurants, gyms)

The pattern comes from product teams tired of dashboards. An agent runs on a schedule, reads a KPI sheet plus its own previous post, and publishes a short summary to Slack with causal framing: not "support volume up 23 percent" but "support volume up 23 percent, highest week-over-week since January, concentrated in Salesforce connector issues." LogRocket has the writeup and Cotera has the exact workflow.

At an independent restaurant, a Monday 7 AM cron pulls Toast or Square POS plus 7shifts labor data. The owner gets one text per location: "labor ran 34 percent versus 28 percent target, driven by Friday and Saturday bar overstaffing, Tuesday covers down 18 percent for the fourth week in a row." Stack is the POS API or a CSV drop plus Claude plus Make or n8n plus Twilio, about $40–60 a month. Replaces the Monday-morning recap the GM writes that nobody reads. Pitch: you already own the POS; the conclusions show up on your phone before you unlock the door. For a pilates or yoga studio, swap in Mindbody and Mariana Tek — the owner gets a Monday 6 AM text about class fill, signups, and which instructor's classes are growing. Around $30 a month.

3. The standup that chases itself (trades, home health)

A Slack agent that does what a Google Form can't: prompts each person on a schedule, tracks who replied, DMs non-responders after 45 minutes, counts skips across the week, and on the third skip pings the manager with the specific pattern. It remembers. Cotera has the full writeup. https://cotera.co/articles/slack-workflow-builder-advanced-guide

At a 10-tech HVAC shop, it's a Friday 4 PM SMS. Each tech gets their week's completed jobs pre-filled from ServiceTitan or Housecall Pro. They reply with notes per job, the agent auto-files into the job record, invoices go out with real notes. Around $80 a month. Replaces the owner texting techs on Sunday night. At a home-health agency, it's an end-of-shift SMS to each visiting nurse with her visit list pre-filled. The agent files notes into WellSky or MatrixCare before she gets to her car. Around $100 a month. Compliance goes up. Retention goes up, because the nurse isn't doing an hour of charting at her kitchen table.

4. A voice agent that answers the phone and books the job (dental and vet clinics, small medical practices, independent restaurants)

Covered in the TL;DR. Source is Owned and Operated #173 with Avoca. https://www.ownedandoperated.com/post/owned-and-operated-173-we-booked-400-calls-a-week-how-avoca-ai-is-shaping-home-services

Healthcare version: SIP forward on ring-no-answer and after-hours at a dental, vet, or small medical practice. Agent reads patient history in Dentrix, Eaglesoft, ezyVet, or Kareo, provider calendar, and rules about insurance and emergencies. Books cleanings and re-exams, routes emergencies to on-call, files refill requests as tasks for the MA. Writes back to the PMS. Vapi or Retell for voice plus Claude plus the PMS API. Around $300–700 a month. Replaces the front-desk person covering evenings plus the afternoon lull you can't staff. Pitch: you already pay for new-patient acquisition; you're losing the 38 percent who call after five.

Restaurant version: the same agent reads Resy or OpenTable availability, books the table, offers alternates when full. $200–500 a month. Replaces the fifteen reservations a week that walk because the host is on the floor.

5. A pager that notices supply problems before you do (restaurants, small construction)

Crisp announced a CPG supply agent in February that watches distributor feeds, detects low-inventory or velocity spikes, drafts a replenishment recommendation, and emails the retail buyer directly. The interesting part is that it reaches out — enterprise tooling surfaces the same alerts and waits for someone to act.

SMB restaurants have no distributor telemetry, so the direction flips. A daily cron plus a Gmail or SMS watcher on Sysco, US Foods, and specialty supplier threads. Reads Toast velocity on spoilables and the on-hand sheet. When forecast exceeds on-hand for short-shelf-life items, or when a supplier email contains "pushed" or "out of stock," the agent texts the owner or chef with the SKU, the gap, and the actual move: call supplier B, sub this cut, 86 the special tonight. Around $40 a month. Replaces the Sunday inventory spiral and the Wednesday 86-list scramble. At a small general contractor, the same agent watches lumber yard threads and cross-references the project schedule in Buildertrend or Procore. When a delivery slides past the install date, the PM gets a message with the project, the trade, and the crew to reschedule. Around $80 a month.
