Agent Pattern Scan — 2026-10-06 — 4 patterns: missing-event trigger, denial-packet assembler, ERP-native quoting agent, single-agent incident fixer

https://github.com/carrollr01/agent-adoption-research/blob/main/log/2026-10-06.md

Four patterns today. The highest-leverage copy is the "missing event" trigger — agents that watch for work that was finished but not yet billed, instead of reacting to an invoice that already exists.

OpenAI shipped Dots at DevDay 2026 on September 29. The most useful customer example in the launch was a freelancer whose Dot noticed an invoice he had forgotten to send, pulled the project details out of the email thread, drafted the invoice as a PDF, and sent it after one-click approval. The trigger is the absence of something that should have happened, not a new event. For dental and vet clinics this becomes a nightly sweep of the practice management system to flag visits that are clinically complete but have no superbill yet, in time to fix the posting the same week. For small construction and trades, the same shape runs against job management software and flags job phases where completion photos exist but no invoice has been drafted. For freelancers and small professional services firms, it reads Google Calendar plus a time-tracking tool plus Gmail and surfaces unbilled work older than N days. The pitch worth testing on a discovery call: "you don't need better invoicing, you need something that notices you forgot."

CallSphere and the broader denial-automation wave running through 2026 both land on the same mechanism, which is worth stealing. A Medicare redetermination packet traditionally takes about two hours and ten minutes because 90% of the work is fetching documents from six systems that don't talk to each other — the clearinghouse worklist, the practice management system, the EHR chart, the payer portal, the coverage policy, and the Word document. The 2026 agent sits on the denial worklist trigger, fetches all six, assembles the packet with the appeal draft on top, and hands it to a specialist for a go/no-go. One billing company moved from appealing 84 small-balance denials a month to 391 with the same team. The generalizable shape is siloed-document aggregation for a repetitive compliance artifact, and it copies cleanly to dental PPO appeals, insurance commission disputes for independent agencies, home health Medicaid appeals, and physical therapy denials — any field where small-ticket paperwork gets left on the floor because the labor doesn't justify the recovery.

Lark came out of the YC Fall 2026 batch targeting wholesale distributors, and the architectural choice is more interesting than the sector. Instead of asking the operator to codify the unwritten rules of their business (which customer gets what price, which SKU substitutes for which, which late payer still gets credit), Lark's agent plugs into the distributor's ERP, reads the full decision history, and uses that history as its training context for new work. New RFQ emails get parsed, line items get matched against the catalog, pricing comes from actual past decisions rather than a brittle price list, and quotes come back in minutes. The pattern copies to small manufacturers running Fishbowl or Katana, auto parts and electrical/plumbing supply houses that still work off counter sales and voicemail callbacks, and food distribution with its allocation and substitution rules. Pitch: "we don't replace your pricing team, we replace the lag between a customer asking and your team getting around to answering."

Polylane published a note in October 2026 arguing sub-agents are just wrong, and backed it by replacing their own up-to-18-agent autofix pipeline with a single continuous agent that owns the full production-incident loop — watching metrics, logs and traces, building a context graph of system dependencies, correlating related alerts, investigating root cause, and either proposing or applying a fix behind a human approval gate. PRs reportedly arrived faster and cheaper. The architectural note matters for anyone proposing agents at an SMB budget. Handoffs between specialized agents cost more than they save until you're at enterprise scale. For agencies running client applications and small SaaS firms, the same single-agent architecture plugs into Sentry or Datadog and the GitHub repo and drafts a PR or a rollback suggestion into Slack. For a property management company running its own tenant portal, it watches application errors, payment failures and support tickets as a single stream against the last deploy. The one-liner for client calls isn't the mechanism, it's the warning: if your pitch starts with "we deploy nine specialized agents," your prospect's small team will spend more time managing the swarm than they would have spent on the original problem.
