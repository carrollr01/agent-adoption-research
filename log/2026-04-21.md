# 2026-04-21 — Agent Pattern Scan

## TL;DR
Three patterns today. The one worth acting on is Corvera, a YC W26 company whose agents parse inbound purchase order PDFs and emails, resolve the line items against a brand's SKU catalog, and execute fulfillment end-to-end with human-in-the-loop approval. Direct Sollos fit: every new distributor order Stephen gets shows up as an unstructured email thread, and the copy-paste between that inbox and Shopify is exactly the task this agent eats. Also carrying: MarketMan's April 2 invoice-scanning integration with Square (photo of the invoice becomes structured inventory data), and CollegeVine's Rec Letter Assistant as the cleanest live example of drafting in a specific person's voice from their prior-writing corpus.

## Patterns

### 1. The inbound order desk — `sollos`
**Source:** [Corvera on YC](https://www.ycombinator.com/companies/corvera), [Launch YC: Corvera](https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/PES-corvera-ai-ops-for-cpg-brands) — YC W26 company; went from $0 to $33K MRR in four weeks across 12 CPG brands as of mid-March; 130% week-over-week growth.

**Mechanism:** An incoming email with a purchase order attached (PDF or in the body text) hits a shared brand inbox. The agent parses the thread, extracts line items, resolves each line against the brand's product catalog — "12 x YM Classic 16oz" gets matched to the right SKU even when a retailer uses their own naming convention — and executes the downstream workflow: draft an order confirmation, file the shipment request in Shopify or a 3PL, kick off an invoice, track payment. Anything the agent isn't sure about stops for human approval. It replaces the work of a back-office ops coordinator, the role a founder-led CPG brand hires around the time the inbox gets unmanageable.

**Why agentic:** The interesting piece is entity resolution across unstructured buyer language, not just "generate text." It also executes downstream actions in multiple systems (inbox, Shopify, accounting) and knows when to stop and ask. A dashboard would display the orders and wait for a human to act; this does the work.

**SMB translation:** Sollos is the exact shape. Stephen is already fielding inbound orders from small grocers and cafes as one-off emails, and every one of them has to get copied into Shopify by hand. Deploy an agent on a shared orders@ Gmail label — it reads the thread, resolves the line items against the Sollos SKU list, drafts a confirmation in Stephen's voice, and files the shipment request in Shopify. It asks for approval on any new buyer or on anything above a dollar threshold. Stack: Gmail + Claude + a Shopify MCP + a catalog/entity-resolution layer, $150–300 a month at Sollos's current volume. Replaces the 60–90 minutes a day Stephen doesn't have. Pitch: you don't need to hire an ops person yet — you need an agent that does the copy-paste between your inbox and Shopify and stops to ask when it hits something new.

This is the mirror image of the Crisp pattern from 2026-04-17. That one was supply-side outbound — a brand reaching into its retailers' telemetry. Corvera is demand-side inbound — orders arriving in your email as unstructured text and PDFs, needing to get into structured systems.

### 2. The photo of the invoice becomes the invoice — `restaurants`, `landscaping`, `hvac`
**Source:** [BusinessWire press release, April 2](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260402238712/en/Square-and-MarketMan-Launch-Advanced-Inventory-Management-Integration-for-Restaurants), [MarketMan product page](https://www.marketman.com/blog/ai-invoice-scanning-from-marketman-faster-smarter-and-made-for-restaurants) — Square and MarketMan shipped a joint integration on April 2; MarketMan's invoice-scanning is the mechanism worth copying.

**Mechanism:** A chef or owner takes a photo of a paper supplier invoice, or forwards a PDF by email, or drops it in a shared folder. MarketMan's models — custom-tuned per supplier's invoice format, SKU list, and pricing — extract the line items, match each to a known SKU in the restaurant's catalog, update on-hand inventory, recalculate recipe costs against POS sales, and flag any line that looks off. When the model is 100% sure on every line, the invoice is processed the moment it's scanned. Anything it's uncertain about goes to a review queue.

**Why agentic:** The trigger is an unscheduled, physical event — a delivery lands and a phone photo fires the workflow. The agent reads an image, does entity resolution against the restaurant's own catalog, writes to inventory, and watches for anomalies (a unit price that jumped 8% since last week). It's event-driven and it takes action; the dashboard version of this would be the spreadsheet that gets updated three days later, if at all.

**SMB translation:** You don't have to be on Square or MarketMan for this. The pattern runs on off-the-shelf parts. An independent restaurant owner sets a Gmail forward rule on invoices@theirdomain; Claude with vision reads the PDF or photo, writes a row into a Google Sheet with vendor, date, SKU, quantity, unit price, and flags any line where unit price moved more than 5% from the last delivery of that SKU. Same shape works for a landscaping company buying fertilizer and nursery stock, or a small HVAC shop getting distributor invoices the warehouse manager used to staple to a clipboard. Stack: Gmail + Claude + Google Sheets + a small catalog file + a Friday digest SMS, $40–70 a month. Replaces about 90 minutes a week of hand-entering line items plus catches the price creep nobody was tracking. Pitch: take a picture of the invoice. Your on-hand sheet is updated before the truck pulls out, and you find out about price hikes before your margin does.

Different in direction from the 2026-04-17 supply-pager pattern, which watched *outgoing* supplier emails for delay language. This one fires *after* a delivery lands and catches price and quantity errors — the thing nobody notices until the month-end P&L.

### 3. Drafting in the counselor's actual voice — `jenn`
**Source:** [CollegeVine Rec Letter Assistant](https://www.collegevine.com/rec-letters), [CollegeVine blog on using AI for rec letters](https://blog.collegevine.com/should-you-use-ai-for-student-rec-letters) — the clearest live example of voice-fingerprinted drafting.

**Mechanism:** The counselor uploads two or three of her own prior rec letters and a student's brag sheet. The agent produces a draft in the counselor's voice — cadence, specific turns of phrase, her transitional tics — populated with the student's specifics. The corpus is used as a style reference, not as content. The output is a draft the counselor edits, not a chatbot conversation. The mechanism is: prior writing corpus + structured facts about a subject + a prompt that treats the corpus as style grounding.

**Why agentic:** The flow is triggered by assignment (a student moves into application season), the output lands in the right place as a draft, and the voice-matching is doing real work that a template can't. It's not a chat — it's a workflow that fires on a lifecycle event and puts a usable artifact in the counselor's hands.

**SMB translation:** Jenn writes between 30 and 60 letters of support, supplements, and follow-up emails to admissions officers each fall, on behalf of students she's worked with for years. Today she does it blank page at 9 pm. Build: a small web form or a labeled Google Doc folder. She tags a student, the agent pulls that student's file from her CRM or her Notion plus the last 40 letters she's written as voice reference, and outputs a draft she edits instead of writes. Stack: Claude + a folder of her prior letters + her student database + a simple form, $80–120 a month. Replaces 4–6 hours a week during application season. Pitch: your letters are the part of your work that nobody else can copy. The agent does the typing so you can do the thinking.

Generalizes cleanly to the other active verticals. Justin at The Music Space writes recital-prep and parent update emails in a recognizable voice; same pattern. Stephen at Sollos writes investor and buyer update emails; same pattern. Debbie at Capital Educators writes progress reports to parents; same pattern. In each case the owner already owns the voice corpus — it's sitting in their Gmail sent folder. The moat is the corpus. The agent just unlocks it.
