# 2026-10-06 — Agent Pattern Scan

## TL;DR
Four patterns worth logging today. The one with the clearest copy into under-served sectors is the "missing event" trigger — an agent that watches the gap between work completed and work billed, instead of reacting to a billed invoice after the fact. Honorable mention to the medical-denial packet assembler, which turns a two-hour document-chase across six systems into a one-click review.

## Patterns

### 1. The "work finished but not invoiced" detector
*(tags: `professional-services`, `trades`, `small-construction`, `freelancers`, `dental-and-vet-clinics`)*

OpenAI's [Dots launch at DevDay 2026](https://thenextweb.com/news/openai-dots-always-on-ai-agents-cloud-computers-devday) shipped on September 29 with an always-on persistent agent architecture, and the single most illustrative customer example wasn't a brand new workflow — it was an agent that noticed an invoice its owner had forgotten to send. The freelance writer who tested it had completed work that was logged in an email thread but never billed. The dot ran a read-only sweep across his connected apps on a quiet interval, matched completed-work signals in his inbox to the absence of a matching invoice, pulled the relevant project and payment details from the thread, drafted the invoice as a PDF, and waited for his one-click approval before sending. The agent's trigger isn't an event, it's the *absence* of an event that should have happened by now.

That inversion is what makes this worth copying. Every pattern in our prior 14 days of logs reacts to something that happened (invoice created, milestone completed, call missed, review posted). This one reacts to something that didn't happen. For dental and vet clinics, the same shape works against the back-end of the day: an agent runs at 7pm against the practice management system (Dentrix, Eaglesoft, Covetrus Pulse), pulls every visit marked complete, cross-references the superbill queue, and surfaces any visit that was closed clinically but has no coded encounter yet. Stack: PM API + Google Workspace + Claude or GPT-5-class model for coding suggestions + Twilio for optional staff SMS. The job it replaces is the office manager's Monday-morning catch-up pass through unposted charges, which is where most lost revenue in small practices hides. Pitch for the discovery call: "every night, before you close the office, we tell you which visits haven't made it to a bill — in time to fix it the same week, not the next audit."

The same mechanism translates nicely to small construction and trades. An agent runs on a cron against job management software (BuilderTREND, CoConstruct, Jobber, Housecall Pro) and the project email thread, finds jobs where photos and completion notes exist but no invoice has been generated for the phase, and drafts the invoice with the completion photos attached and the owner's standard payment terms. For freelancers and small professional services firms, the trigger reads Google Calendar + a time-tracking tool like Harvest or Toggl + Gmail, and flags unbilled work older than N days. Pitch: "you don't need better invoicing — you need something that notices you forgot."

### 2. The medical denial that assembles its own appeal packet
*(tags: `small-medical-practices`, `dental-and-vet-clinics`, `home-health`, `physical-therapy`, `optometry`)*

[CallSphere's founder Sagar Shankaran](https://callsphere.ai/blog/two-hours-to-build-one-redetermination-packet-now-you-hand-over-the-go) and the broader denials-automation wave running through 2026 both land on the same mechanism, and it's a good one. A Medicare redetermination packet traditionally takes about two hours and ten minutes of billing-specialist time because 90% of the work is fetching documents from systems that don't talk to each other — the clearinghouse worklist for the remit, the practice management system for the claim history, the EHR for the chart note, the payer portal for the specific coverage policy, and finally Word for the appeal letter itself. The 2026 agent sits on the denial-worklist trigger, fetches all six artifacts automatically, assembles them into a HIPAA-compliant packet with the appeal draft on top, and hands the whole thing to a specialist for a go/no-go review. One billing company moved from appealing 84 small-balance denials a month to 391 using the same team. [Anthropic's Claude Cowork](https://www.accelirate.com/accelirate-announces-the-launch-of-a-new-scalable-product-for-denials-and-appeals-management-in-healthcare/), released earlier in 2026, is the general-purpose version of the same move: give it a goal, it fans out across your connected files and systems, and it hands back a finished document packet.

The generalizable shape is "siloed-document aggregation for a repetitive compliance artifact," and the under-served sectors are easy to find. For dental and vet clinics, the exact same packet pattern applies to insurance pre-authorizations and dental PPO appeals, which small practices routinely let drop because the per-case margin doesn't justify the two hours. Stack: PM software + Google Drive or Dropbox for chart documents + Claude or Cowork + a review-approval UI in Google Sheets or a lightweight Next.js dashboard. Replaces the office manager's lowest-value Friday afternoon work. Pitch: "we don't write your appeals, we fetch everything you need and lay it on your desk ready to sign."

Beyond healthcare, the same pattern copies to any field where small-ticket paperwork is left on the floor because the labor doesn't justify the recovery. For independent insurance agencies, the trigger is a carrier denial or a short-pay on a commission statement, and the agent assembles the policy history, the application, the carrier correspondence thread, and the commission schedule into a dispute packet. For home health agencies, the trigger is a Medicaid or private insurer denial, and the agent pulls aide visit notes, care-plan documentation, and 485 forms into the appeal packet. For physical therapy and optometry, the mechanism is identical. The one-liner: "your lowest-margin work is the work your team doesn't finish — let the fetching happen without them."

### 3. The ERP-native agent that learned your pricing by watching
*(tags: `wholesale-distributors`, `small-manufacturers`, `electrical-and-plumbing-supply`, `food-distribution`, `auto-parts`)*

[Lark](https://dealroom.co/just-founded/lark/) is a Y Combinator Fall 2026 company building AI agents specifically for wholesale distributors — a sector that still runs on legacy ERPs (Epicor, SAP Business One, Infor, Acumatica) and manual email-based RFQ handling. The founders' bet is that distributor back-offices look repetitive on the surface but are actually full of unwritten rules — which customer gets what price, which SKU gets substituted for which other SKU, which late payer gets credit anyway because the owner knows the family. Instead of asking the operator to codify those rules, Lark's agent plugs into the ERP, reads the full history of orders, quotes, approvals, overrides and credit decisions, and uses that history as its training context for new work. New RFQ emails get parsed, line items get matched against the catalog, customer-specific pricing gets pulled from the actual decision history rather than from a brittle price list, and the quote comes back in minutes. The approvals that previously flowed through a quotes coordinator now flow through a Slack approval or a daily email digest.

The thing to steal here isn't the sector — it's the architecture. Prior logs have patterns that scan listings (5/23 Kim Howard real estate agent) and patterns that respond to events in known SaaS tools (5/25 Anthropic invoice chase, 5/28 milestone invoicing). This one grounds itself in a specific operator's past decisions as the authoritative source of what to do next. For small manufacturers running MRP systems like Fishbowl or Katana, the same trigger — a new quote request landing in email or a customer portal — reads the shop's own past quotes for similar part geometries, including which jobs the owner declined, and drafts a quote that reflects the shop's real pricing posture rather than a theoretical margin table. Stack: ERP or MRP API + the inbox + Claude or an open-source model fine-tuned on recent decisions + a Retool or internal Next.js approval screen. Replaces the owner's son or the office manager who currently writes 60% of quotes.

For auto parts and electrical/plumbing supply houses — which look like 1998 inside and have razor margins — the same agent handles the counter-sale callback: a contractor leaves a message asking if a part is in stock, the agent checks inventory, pulls that contractor's prior order history for the house's standard pricing to that account, and texts back a quote within three minutes. The pitch worth testing: "we don't replace your pricing team, we replace the lag between a customer asking and your team getting around to answering." For food distribution and specialty wholesalers managing allocation and substitution rules the pattern holds exactly.

### 4. The one-agent production incident fixer
*(tags: `software-vendors`, `small-saas-firms`, `agencies-running-client-apps`, `property-management`, `logistics-platforms`)*

[Polylane](https://poweredbyai.app/project/polylane) is interesting less for what it does and more for what it chose *not* to do. Production incident response is the canonical multi-agent demo — a planner agent reads the alert, an investigator agent reads the logs, a diagnoser agent reads the code, a patcher agent writes the fix, a reviewer agent checks the diff, and on and on. Polylane's October 2026 post ["Sub-agents are just wrong"](https://polylane.com/blog/sub-agents-are-just-wrong/) argues the handoffs between those sub-agents are where real incidents actually get lost, and they replaced an up-to-18-agent autofix pipeline with a single continuous agent that owns the whole loop: watches the metrics, logs and traces for anomalies, builds a context graph of system dependencies, correlates related alerts to collapse noise, investigates the root cause against the codebase, and either proposes or applies a fix behind a human approval gate — PRs reportedly arrived faster and cheaper. The architectural choice matters for anyone building agents at an SMB budget: handoffs cost more than they save until you're at enterprise scale.

For agencies that run client applications and small SaaS firms, this is directly deployable. The trigger is an alert from the monitoring stack (Sentry, Datadog, Honeybadger, Better Stack); the context the agent reads is the repository, the metric history, and the last N deploys; the action is a draft PR or a one-click rollback suggestion sitting in Slack. Stack: GitHub + Sentry or Datadog webhook + Claude or an Anthropic Managed Agent + a Slack approval. Replaces the on-call rotation for the 70% of incidents that are "same thing broke again." Pitch to a small SaaS owner: "you'll keep the on-call rotation for the hard things — this one takes the 2am 'disk full on the queue worker' off your calendar."

The same single-agent-over-sub-agents idea generalizes beyond software ops. For a property management company that runs its own tenant portal, an agent watches application errors, payment failures, and support tickets as a single stream, correlates them against the last code or settings change, and surfaces the fix path to the one developer the company employs part-time. For small logistics platforms, the same architecture watches the stream of EDI errors, carrier rejections, and dispatch anomalies, and treats them as one problem with one owner instead of fanning them to separate inboxes. The one-liner isn't the mechanism, it's the architectural warning: if your pitch starts with "we deploy nine specialized agents," your prospect's small team will spend more time managing the swarm than they would have spent on the original problem.
