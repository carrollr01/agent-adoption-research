Agent Pattern Scan — 2026-07-16 — 3 patterns | scout-then-expert code review architecture

Three patterns worth stealing today. The interesting mechanism is DoorDash's code reviewer — copy the architecture, not the domain.

Scout-then-expert two-tier review. DoorDash's production code reviewer runs 10,000+ pull requests a week across 56 repos. A cheap Claude Sonnet 4.6 scout reads the full diff and flags a shortlist of suspicious spots. Two Claude Opus 4.8 deep reviewers verify only those spots and post inline PR comments. Result: 504 real findings at 53.6% weighted recall versus 164 findings and 30.7% for single-pass GPT baselines, at about $3.91 per PR. They also built DashBench, which replays historical PRs and scores whether the agent surfaces findings humans actually acted on — that's what earned engineer trust. The generalizable trick is not "get an agent to review things," it's "use a cheap model to narrow the search space so the expensive model only runs where it matters." This copies straight into accounting firms doing tax return QA in Drake or Lacerte during March, dental clinics reviewing insurance claim submissions in Dentrix before biller submit, or P&C insurance adjusters on inbound claims. The pitch to a CPA firm: your seniors stop scanning returns line-by-line and start reviewing only the seven items per return that actually matter.

Custom agents replacing the SMB SaaS stack. Time profiled Spencer Handley of Sonora, an online guitar school that cut its team from 48 to 30 while keeping revenue flat by replacing HubSpot, Calendly, Vimeo, and DocuSign with custom agentic workflows on Claude Opus 4.5. The outreach team was eliminated (an outbound agent runs sequenced messages and books discovery calls), the onboarding coordinator was eliminated (an agent provisions students, schedules intakes, mails welcome kits, and hosts lessons), the ops coordinator was eliminated (an agent handles reschedules, refunds, and enrollment changes from a shared inbox). Annual savings around $250K. The generalizable pattern: when a bundle of SaaS tools each costs $100–500 a month and you use maybe 10% of what they do, an agent is the thin thing that does the 10% you actually use, with your CRM being a Sheet or a Postgres table you own. Tutoring franchises, small marketing agencies, and coaches are the obvious ports. The pitch: you're paying $6K a year for HubSpot, Calendly, and Loom to do things a $500-a-month agent could handle better, because the agent knows your specific clients and services and those tools don't.

Describe-a-process, get an approvable workflow. Rewst rolled out an AI Agent tool where the MSP owner types a description of a process — "when a new client signs the MSA in DocuSign, provision their Microsoft 365 tenant, send onboarding email, and schedule the kickoff" — and the agent generates a full runnable workflow the human can eyeball and approve before it goes live. The interesting shift is who does the authoring: no more needing a Rewst specialist, the domain expert describes it in English. This unlocks sectors where SMB owners have the domain knowledge but no interest in a visual workflow tool — home health agencies describing visit-close automations, restaurant groups describing labor-alert workflows. For A3 Dev the read is that description-based authoring is coming to every workflow tool this year. The value shifts from "we can build in n8n or Make" to "we know which processes are worth automating for you, what data feeds the trigger, and where the approval gate should sit."
