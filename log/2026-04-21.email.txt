Three patterns today after three empty days. The one worth acting on first is Corvera, a YC W26 company whose agents parse inbound purchase orders (PDF or email) and process them end-to-end with human approval. It's the mirror of the Crisp pattern from April 17 — not a brand reaching into its retailers' telemetry, but orders arriving in the brand's own inbox as unstructured text and getting parsed into Shopify. Direct Sollos fit: every new distributor order Stephen gets shows up as an email thread, and the copy-paste between that inbox and Shopify is exactly the task this eats. In rough order of how excited I am:

1. The inbound order desk (Sollos)

Corvera launched out of YC W26 and went from $0 to $33K MRR in four weeks across 12 CPG brands, growing 130% week-over-week as of mid-March. https://www.ycombinator.com/launches/PES-corvera-ai-ops-for-cpg-brands

An incoming email with a purchase order attached hits a shared brand inbox. The agent parses the thread, extracts line items, resolves each one against the brand's SKU catalog — "12 x YM Classic 16oz" gets matched even when the retailer uses their own naming convention — drafts an order confirmation, files the shipment request in Shopify or a 3PL, kicks off an invoice, tracks payment. It stops for human approval on anything new or ambiguous. It replaces the back-office ops coordinator a founder-led CPG brand hires when the inbox gets unmanageable.

The interesting piece is entity resolution across unstructured buyer language. That's the actual hard part of the job; everything else is plumbing.

Sollos fit is direct. Stephen is already fielding inbound orders from small grocers and cafes as one-off emails. Deploy an agent on a shared orders@ Gmail label — it reads the thread, resolves line items against the Sollos SKU list, drafts a confirmation in Stephen's voice, and files the shipment request in Shopify. It asks for approval on any new buyer or on anything above a dollar threshold. Stack: Gmail + Claude + a Shopify MCP + a catalog/entity-resolution layer, $150–300 a month at current volume. Replaces the 60–90 minutes a day Stephen doesn't have. Pitch: you don't need to hire an ops person yet — you need an agent that does the copy-paste between your inbox and Shopify and stops to ask when it hits something new.

This is materially different from the April 17 Crisp pattern. That one was supply-side outbound (a brand reaching into its retailers' telemetry and emailing buyers). Corvera is demand-side inbound — orders arriving in your email as unstructured text and needing to get into structured systems.

2. The photo of the invoice becomes the invoice (restaurants, landscaping, HVAC)

Square and MarketMan shipped a joint integration on April 2 that bakes AI invoice scanning into the Square POS workflow. https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260402238712/en/Square-and-MarketMan-Launch-Advanced-Inventory-Management-Integration-for-Restaurants

The mechanism: a chef takes a photo of a paper supplier invoice, or forwards a PDF, or drops it in a shared folder. MarketMan's models — custom-tuned per supplier's invoice format, SKU list, and pricing — extract line items, match each to a known SKU in the restaurant's catalog, update on-hand inventory, recalculate recipe costs against POS sales, and flag any line that looks off. When the model is 100% sure on every line, it processes instantly. Anything uncertain goes to review.

The trigger matters. This isn't an end-of-week reconciliation. It fires the moment the photo hits. The chef's phone became the data-entry device.

For our cold-outreach verticals, the same shape runs on off-the-shelf parts. An independent restaurant owner sets a forward rule on invoices@theirdomain; Claude with vision reads the PDF or photo, writes a row into a Google Sheet with vendor, date, SKU, quantity, unit price, and flags anything where unit price moved more than 5% from the last delivery of that SKU. Same shape for a landscaping company buying fertilizer and nursery stock, or an HVAC shop getting distributor invoices the warehouse manager used to staple to a clipboard. Stack: Gmail + Claude + Google Sheets + a small catalog file + a Friday digest SMS, $40–70 a month. Replaces about 90 minutes a week of hand-entry plus catches the price creep nobody is tracking. Pitch: take a picture of the invoice. Your on-hand sheet is updated before the truck pulls out, and you find out about price hikes before your margin does.

Different in direction from the April 17 supply-pager pattern, which watched outgoing supplier emails for delay language. This one fires after a delivery lands, and the thing it catches is price and quantity errors — the stuff nobody notices until month-end P&L.

3. Drafting in the counselor's actual voice (Jenn, and generalizes to every owner-led vertical)

CollegeVine's Rec Letter Assistant is the clearest in-the-wild example of voice-fingerprinted drafting. https://www.collegevine.com/rec-letters

The counselor uploads two or three prior rec letters and a student's brag sheet. The agent outputs a draft in the counselor's voice — cadence, turns of phrase, transitional tics — populated with the student's specifics. The corpus is used as a style reference, not as content. The mechanism is prior writing corpus + structured facts about a subject + a prompt that treats the corpus as style grounding. What makes it agentic rather than a chatbot is that the flow is triggered by a lifecycle event (a student moves into application season) and the output lands in the right place as a draft the counselor edits, not a chat transcript.

Jenn writes 30 to 60 letters of support, supplements, and admissions-officer follow-ups each fall. Blank page at 9 pm. Build: a small web form or a labeled Google Doc folder. She tags a student, the agent pulls that student's file plus the last 40 letters she's written as voice reference, and outputs a draft she edits instead of writes. Stack: Claude + a folder of her prior letters + her student database + a simple form, $80–120 a month. Replaces 4–6 hours a week during application season. Pitch: your letters are the part of your work that nobody else can copy. The agent does the typing so you can do the thinking.

Generalizes cleanly. Justin writes recital-prep and parent update emails in a recognizable voice. Stephen writes investor and buyer update emails. Debbie writes parent progress reports. Each owner already owns the voice corpus — it's in their sent folder. The moat is the corpus. The agent just unlocks it.
