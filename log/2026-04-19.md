# 2026-04-19 — Agent Pattern Scan

## TL;DR
Faraday is quietly running an agent that files permits, registers warranties, and submits utility rebates for HVAC and plumbing shops by driving the actual government and manufacturer portals. That is a new primitive: agents that fill someone else's forms, not agents that draft a message. The copies are everywhere people drown in portal paperwork — small insurance brokers, medical billers, immigration paralegals, franchise compliance shops.

## Patterns

### 1. The agent that files the paperwork nobody wants to file
*(tags: `trades`, `small-insurance-brokers`, `medical-billing`, `franchise-ops`)*

Faraday is a Y Combinator company targeting the home-services back office — the part of an HVAC, plumbing, or electrical shop whose entire job is chasing permits, warranty registrations, and utility rebates after the truck leaves the driveway. Their pitch, visible across their Feb 2026 hiring posts, is an agent that reads the completed job record in ServiceTitan or Housecall Pro, then drives the actual city permit portal, the manufacturer warranty site, and the utility rebate form to file everything, track status, and write the confirmations back into the FSM record so the invoice doesn't sit in AR waiting on a rebate code ([Faraday via HN Hiring, Feb 2026](https://hnhiring.com/february-2026)).

The reason this matters is that every pattern we've logged so far has the agent doing something inside a system the SMB already owns — draft a Gmail reply, post to Slack, update a job record. This one goes the other way. The agent navigates a third-party portal on behalf of the shop, which is the closest thing to a robot employee most of these businesses will ever encounter. It's a different primitive and it opens a different door.

Drop the same shape into a small independent insurance broker and the trigger is a new bound policy in Applied Epic or EZLynx. The agent reads the policy and the carrier's filing requirements, logs into the state surplus-lines portal, files the stamping, pays the fee, and writes the receipt and policy number back into the agency management system. Stack is Applied Epic or EZLynx plus a headless browser plus Claude plus a small state-by-state rules file that a partner maintains. Around $400–800 a month at a 4–6 producer agency. Replaces the CSR role whose entire job is compliance filing. Pitch: your CSR is a highly paid form-filler; she should be calling renewals, not logging into portals.

Drop it into a two-person medical billing office and the trigger is a new CMS-1500 or superbill arriving from a client practice. The agent reads the claim, checks the payer's portal rules, submits, watches for denial, and either re-submits or flags the specific CARC/RARC code for the human biller. Around $300–600 a month. Replaces an hour a day of portal-hopping per practice served. Same shape works for immigration paralegals filing I-129s and I-140s, franchise-compliance shops filing local business licenses, and accountants filing state registrations for their small-business clients.

### 2. The accounts-receivable chaser that lives inside the ledger
*(tags: `accountants`, `small-professional-services`, `agencies`)*

Xero announced a multi-year deal with Anthropic in March 2026 to build JAX, an agent layer that lives inside their accounting platform ([Xero press release](https://www.xero.com/us/media-releases/xero-and-anthropic-collaborate/)). The mechanism described is the one every SMB needs: the agent watches for an invoice crossing an aging threshold, reads the ledger — invoices, payments, bank feed, and the customer's entire prior communication history on that specific account — and drafts a chase email grounded in what was actually said last time. It doesn't say "your invoice is overdue"; it says "last time we spoke you asked for Net-45; we're now at Net-52." The owner approves in one click inside Xero.

The interesting part is that the grounding material is the ledger itself. Every piece of context the agent needs is already in the system of record. This is why the pattern works at SMB scale where a general-purpose email assistant doesn't — the rules and the history come free.

Drop this into a 3-partner CPA firm that sells monthly advisory engagements. Trigger is an aging invoice in QuickBooks or Xero. The agent reads the engagement letter, the prior-year scope, the partner's last three emails with that client, and drafts a chase in the partner's voice referencing the specific work that month — "the March close and the 1099 run." Stack is the accounting API plus Claude plus the firm's email platform, around $150–300 a month. Replaces the partner's Friday 5 PM sweep through accounts and the awkward first-nudge email. Pitch: the worst part of running a small firm is asking a client you like to pay you; this does the asking in your voice and you hit approve.

Same shape works at a small creative agency. Trigger is an overdue invoice in Harvest or FreshBooks, and the agent reads the project Slack channel and the original SOW so the chase references the actual deliverable. Around $100 a month. Replaces the account manager's least-favorite Monday.

### 3. The outbound caller that hits a lead inside two minutes
*(tags: `real-estate-investors`, `insurance-agencies`, `solar-and-roofing`, `home-services`)*

This one is the counterpart to last week's Avoca pattern. Avoca is an inbound voice agent — it picks up when you call the shop. ISpeedToLead, a marketplace for motivated-seller real-estate leads, runs a Retell-built agent that goes the other direction. The moment a lead lands — web form, aggregator push, missed call — the agent places an outbound call within seconds, qualifies the seller against the buyer's rules, and either books a callback or does a live transfer to the human closer ([Retell case study](https://www.retellai.com/case-study/ispeedtolead-retell-ai-lead-response-case-study)). Dispositions are written back to the CRM.

The reason this matters outside real-estate investing is that the 2-minute-call-back window is a known win in every sector that pays for leads and then loses them to whichever competitor called first. The agent doesn't replace a salesperson. It replaces the night-and-weekend shift, the lunch break, and the lead that sat in a queue for three hours until somebody noticed it.

Drop this into an independent insurance agency buying Medicare Advantage leads at $40–80 each. Trigger is a new lead dropped into EZLynx, AgencyBloc, or a Google Form. The agent calls immediately, confirms the prospect's ZIP and current plan, books a 15-minute slot on the licensed agent's calendar, and files disposition. Around $500–900 a month at 200–400 leads a week. Replaces the junior assistant who's supposed to dial but is usually on another call. Pitch: the $60 lead you bought yesterday is already cold; you're not paying Humana more than your competitors, you're just slower on the phone.

Drop it into a roofing contractor buying from Angi, Thumbtack, or Google LSAs. Same trigger. The agent confirms the property type, rough roof age, and insurance-claim status, then books the estimator. Around $400–700 a month. Replaces the dispatcher who tries to triage leads between answering the office line.

### 4. The SMS leasing agent that books tours from the first text
*(tags: `property-management`, `small-landlords`)*

ShowDigs publishes a concrete writeup of their SMS leasing agent that runs from a prospect's first inquiry on Zillow or Apartments.com through a booked, agent-routed tour ([ShowDigs](https://www.showdigs.com/property-managers/ai-leasing-agent-from-first-text-to-scheduled-tour-automatically)). The trigger is a text or listing inquiry. The agent reads the listing record in AppFolio, Buildium, or Rent Manager — price, availability, qualification rules for income, move-in date, pets, occupants — plus the showing calendar and the leasing agent's route for the day. It texts back, qualifies the prospect, answers questions about the property, proposes tour slots that actually fit the agent's driving route, and books the tour. Edge cases escalate to a human.

The interesting part here isn't the chat. It's the routing awareness. The agent isn't booking against a calendar in the abstract; it's booking against a geographic route, which is the thing every multi-listing PM spends Monday morning untangling. Combine that with the qualification rules happening up front and the pattern does a genuine leasing agent's job, not a receptionist's.

This is already deployed at multi-hundred-unit PMs but the mechanism scales down. At an independent landlord with 20 units, the same agent plugs into a shared Twilio number and a Google Sheet of vacancies, with tours booked on the owner's Google Calendar. Around $80–150 a month. Replaces the owner-operator's evening phone tag with prospects. Pitch: you already miss half of these texts by Wednesday; the ones you answer, you answer in a way that doesn't pre-qualify anyone, so you tour people who can't actually pay.

Adjacent sector: self-storage. Trigger is a web inquiry on a SiteLink or storEDGE facility page. The agent texts, quotes availability, quotes price, handles the reservation and the move-in appointment. Around $100 a month per facility. Replaces the front-desk person covering evenings and Sundays.

### 5. The morning agent that reads four apps and drafts the replies
*(tags: `solo-and-two-person-firms`, `professional-services`, `agencies`)*

JJ Englert at Tenex walked through a pattern on How I AI that's worth stealing if you run a small professional services firm ([Lenny's](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/claude-cowork-101-how-to-automate)). The setup is Claude's scheduled Cowork mode plus a file called `brain.md` sitting in a project folder. `brain.md` is a short, maintained markdown file: who the clients are, who the team is, the voice the owner writes in, the standing priorities for the quarter. At 7 AM the agent runs on a schedule, reads Gmail, Google Calendar, Slack, and Notion through Claude's connectors, filters everything through `brain.md`, drafts Gmail replies in the owner's voice, and posts a one-screen briefing with the day's top three priorities.

The reason this doesn't collapse into "ChatGPT read my email" is the `brain.md` file. It is the thing that makes the agent sound like the owner and prioritize the way the owner would. It's also the thing an SMB can actually maintain — not a CRM schema or a fine-tune, just a document the owner edits every few weeks.

Drop this into a 3-person law firm. The connectors become Gmail, Google Calendar, Slack, and Clio. `brain.md` carries the partners' voices, the matter-by-matter rules about what goes out as a draft versus a hold, and the client sensitivities. Stack is Claude Cowork plus a shared Google Drive folder, around $100–200 a month plus seat cost. Replaces the legal assistant's first-two-hours of the day. Pitch: your paralegal spends ninety minutes on inbox triage; this hands her drafts and an agenda instead, and she spends those ninety minutes on the matter.

Drop it into a 2-person bookkeeping firm. Connectors are Gmail, Calendar, Slack, and QuickBooks Online. `brain.md` carries the client list, the month-end rhythm, and the partner's standard responses to common questions. Around $100 a month. Replaces the partner opening four apps at 8 AM to figure out what the day looks like.
