# 2026-04-17 — Agent Pattern Scan

## TL;DR
Avoca has proven that a voice agent can book 400 calls a week for an HVAC shop. The exact same mechanism drops into an independent dental, veterinary, or small medical practice — phone-heavy, calendar-constrained, expensive front desk, almost no agent adoption today, and roughly 200,000 practices in the US. That's the highest-leverage copy-paste available right now.

## Patterns

### 1. The Slack reaction is the work order
*(tags: `dental-and-vet-clinics`, `accountants`, `small-medical-practices`)*

At Stripe, an engineer sees a bug report, an ask, or a screenshot in Slack and reacts with a specific emoji. That reaction is the whole trigger. An agent — they call them "minions" — spins up an isolated dev environment, reads the surrounding thread, makes the change, and opens a pull request that links back to the Slack message. No form to fill out, no ticket queue, no decision about where the work belongs. Stripe is shipping around 1,300 PRs a week this way. Steve Kaliski walked through it in detail on the 2026-03-25 episode of How I AI ([Lenny's](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-stripe-built-minionsai-coding)).

The interesting part for us isn't the code generation. It's the trigger: one click, in the tool the operator already lives in. Every knowledge-work SMB has the same shape of problem — stuff arrives by email, sits for two days, and the person who owned it has to reconstruct context before they can act.

Drop this into a small dental or vet office and the trigger becomes a Gmail label on a patient thread. The agent reads the thread, pulls the chart note from Dentrix, Eaglesoft, or ezyVet, and drafts a reply in the provider's voice as a Gmail draft. The MA gets pinged: "draft ready for Mrs. Garcia." Stack is Gmail + a Google Apps Script label watcher + Claude + a thin shim over the PMS. Somewhere around $200–400 a month. Replaces about 90 minutes a day of front-desk triage. The pitch: label the message, get back a draft grounded in the chart. The provider edits or signs. Nobody starts from blank.

The same thing works at a two-partner CPA firm. Label a client email and the agent pulls prior-year return data from Karbon or Canopy, drafts a reply that references the specific notice or line item. Replaces the partner's 7 pm triage session. Pitch: every client email becomes a labeled work order, grounded in their actual file.

### 2. A Monday-morning text that does the work of a Monday-morning report
*(tags: `restaurants`, `gyms`)*

The pattern started with product teams who were tired of dashboards. An agent runs on a schedule, reads a KPI sheet plus the previous week's post it wrote, and publishes a short summary to Slack with causal framing — not just "support volume up 23 percent" but "support volume up 23 percent, highest week-over-week since January, concentrated in the Salesforce connector." LogRocket walked through it recently and Cotera has a clean writeup with the exact workflow ([LogRocket](https://blog.logrocket.com/product-management/ai-powered-slack-workflows-for-product-managers), [Cotera](https://cotera.co/articles/slack-workflow-builder-advanced-guide)).

The reason this matters outside of tech is that most SMB owners are the only person who looks at their own numbers, and they do it by logging into one SaaS or another and squinting. That's where the hour goes.

At an independent restaurant, a Monday 7 AM cron pulls Toast or Square POS plus 7shifts labor data. The owner gets one text per location: "labor ran 34 percent versus 28 percent target, driven by Friday and Saturday bar overstaffing, Tuesday covers down 18 percent for the fourth week in a row, top-selling item swapped from salmon to short-rib burger." Stack is the POS API or a CSV drop plus Claude plus Make or n8n plus Twilio, around $40–60 a month. Replaces the Monday-morning recap the GM writes and nobody reads. Pitch: you already own the POS, stop opening it; the conclusions show up before you unlock the door.

For a pilates or yoga studio, it's Mindbody or Mariana Tek instead, and the owner gets a Monday 6 AM text about class fill, signups, cancellations, and which instructor's classes are growing. Around $30 a month. Replaces an hour of clicking through Mindbody.

### 3. The standup that chases itself
*(tags: `trades`, `home-health`)*

There's a well-documented Slack agent that does what a Google Form can't: it prompts each person at 9 AM, tracks who replied, DMs non-responders at 9:45, and counts skips across the week. On the third skip by the same person, the manager gets a DM with the specific pattern — "Jen skipped Monday, Wednesday, Friday." It's stateful. It remembers. Cotera has the full writeup.

The reason this matters outside of software is that every SMB with a distributed field workforce has the same problem: the owner is also the ops manager, and most of their Sunday evening is chasing people for notes.

At a mid-size trades business — say an HVAC shop with 10 techs — it runs as a Friday 4 PM SMS. Each tech gets their week's completed jobs pre-filled from ServiceTitan or Housecall Pro or Jobber. They reply with notes per job. The agent auto-files the notes into the job record so photos and notes are one tap from the invoice. Nudges at Saturday 10 AM. Pings the ops manager Sunday night with the laggards. Around $80 a month. Replaces the owner texting techs on a Sunday. Pitch: the techs get one SMS with their jobs listed, you stop being the nag, and invoices go out with real notes on them.

At a home-health agency, it's an end-of-shift SMS to each visiting nurse with her day's visit list pre-filled. She replies with notes per patient, and the agent files them into WellSky or MatrixCare before she gets to her car. Around $100 a month. Compliance goes up. So does retention, because the nurse isn't doing an hour of charting at her kitchen table after an eleven-hour day.

### 4. A voice agent that answers the phone and books the job
*(tags: `dental-and-vet-clinics`, `small-medical-practices`, `independent-restaurants`)*

This is the one to pay attention to. Avoca has been quietly running a voice agent for HVAC, plumbing, and electrical shops that books inbound and after-hours calls. At one operator they publicly discussed it was booking 400 calls a week. The agent answers, asks the caller the right questions, checks the tech calendar in ServiceTitan or Housecall Pro, honors service-area and pricing guardrails, books a slot, and sends a confirmation SMS. It also declines or defers when the rules require — it is not a phone tree ([Owned and Operated #173](https://www.ownedandoperated.com/post/owned-and-operated-173-we-booked-400-calls-a-week-how-avoca-ai-is-shaping-home-services)).

This mechanism is the highest-leverage copy available in the agent space right now, because it is already proven in one sector (trades) and the operationally identical sectors next door have almost none of it.

At an independent dental, vet, or small medical practice, the setup is the same. A forwarded number on ring-no-answer and after-hours, an agent that reads patient history in Dentrix, Eaglesoft, ezyVet, or Kareo, the provider calendar, and the rules about insurance and emergencies. It books cleanings, re-exams, and first-available slots, routes emergencies to on-call, and files refill requests as tasks for the MA. Writes everything back to the PMS. Stack is Vapi or Retell for the voice layer plus Claude for the dialogue plus the PMS API. Around $300–700 a month all-in at practice volumes. Replaces the front-desk person covering evenings and the afternoon lull you can't really staff. Pitch: you already pay for new-patient acquisition; you're losing the 38 percent who call after five.

At an independent restaurant, the same mechanism takes reservations. Reads Resy or OpenTable availability and house rules, books the table, offers alternates when full. Around $200–500 a month. Replaces the fifteen reservations a week that walk because the host is on the floor and the landline went to voicemail.

### 5. A pager that notices supply problems before you do
*(tags: `restaurants`, `small-construction`)*

Crisp announced a set of AI agents for CPG brands and retailers in February that watches distributor and retailer inventory feeds, detects when a SKU is running low at a specific distribution center or spiking in velocity, drafts a concrete replenishment recommendation with units and date, and emails the retail buyer directly with the ask. The interesting part isn't the detection. It's that the agent reaches out. Enterprise tooling surfaces the same alerts on a dashboard and waits for someone to act on them ([Crisp](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260211212237/en/Crisp-Launches-AI-Agents-to-Drive-Shareholder-Value-for-CPG-Brands-and-Retailers)).

SMB restaurants don't have distributor telemetry, so the direction flips. Instead of watching feeds from above, you watch the owner's own surfaces: a daily cron plus a Gmail or SMS watcher on Sysco, US Foods, Restaurant Depot, and specialty supplier threads; Toast velocity on spoilable SKUs; the on-hand sheet the kitchen keeps in Google Sheets or MarketMan. When the forecast exceeds what's on hand for something short-shelf-life, or when a supplier email contains delay language — "pushed," "out of stock," "substituting" — the agent drafts a text to the owner or chef with the SKU, the gap, and the actual move. Call supplier B. Sub this cut. 86 the special tonight. Around $40 a month. Replaces the Sunday night inventory spiral and the Wednesday 86-list scramble. Pitch: you don't need an ERP; you need a pager that wakes up before service does.

At a small GC or specialty-trade contractor, the same agent watches lumber yard and supply house threads and cross-references them against the project schedule in Buildertrend, CoConstruct, or Procore. When a delivery slides past the scheduled install date, the PM gets a message naming the project, the trade, and the crew that needs rescheduling. Around $80 a month. Replaces the PM finding out the framing package is three days late when the framers show up.
